How much of a valuation gap is the forecast, and what would it take the discount rate to hide it?

Enter a benchmark rate and two views of the same asset: two forecasts, or a forecast and a price. The page keeps the benchmark rate c fixed and puts the whole gap into one number, the valuation ratio K. From the two it derives the gain of capital g and the effective rate ceff.

What did a price series imply, looking back?

Paste prices or choose a file. For every date that has a price one horizon later, the page computes the realized valuation ratio K: the later price, discounted at the benchmark rate, divided by the price on the date. This is the back-test of the article, run on your own series.

A reading in hindsight: each K uses a price that was not known on its date. Use prices that include reinvested distributions (adjusted close). The file is read in your browser and is not uploaded.

Summary

The equations, term by term

A discounted cash flow value has a numerator, the expected benefits, and a denominator, the discount rate. One rate is usually asked to do two jobs: stand for what the money could earn elsewhere, and soak up doubts about the forecast. The framework gives each job its own number.

Equation numbers are those of the published article.

c benchmark cost of capital

The return given up by putting money in this asset and not in the closest comparable alternative. It comes from outside the asset: it does not move when your forecast of the asset moves.

Vfair benchmark fair value, eq. (7)

V_fair = Σ E[Benefit_s] / (1 + c)^(s − t)

The benchmark forecast of each year's benefit, discounted at c back to today (t) and added up. "Fair" means measured against this benchmark forecast, nothing more.

V the valuation examined, eq. (10)

The same sum with another forecast in the numerator and the same c in the denominator. A market price can stand in for it.

K valuation ratio, eq. (11)

K = V_fair / V

The whole gap between the two views in one number. K above 1: the valuation is below the benchmark value (a pessimistic forecast, or a price below fair value). K below 1: above it. K equal to 1: no gap. If the examined forecast is the benchmark forecast scaled by the same factor every year, K is one over that factor and does not depend on c.

g gain of capital, eqs. (20) and (21)

g = (1 + c)^h · (K − 1)

Over h years the benchmark grows a unit of money to (1 + c)^h. Buying at V what is worth V_fair grows it to (1 + c)^h · K. g is the difference between the two growth factors: 0.16 means 16 cents more per unit invested over the whole horizon, not per year.

ceff effective rate, eqs. (25) and (26)

c_eff = (1 + c) · K^(−1/h) − 1

The single discount rate that, applied to the examined forecast, would return the benchmark fair value. It is what a one-rate DCF would have to use to hide the gap. Eq. (26) is exact for one payoff at the horizon. When benefits arrive year by year, solving the definition, eq. (25), on the yearly amounts gives a somewhat different rate, and the calculator shows both.

K from prices eqs. (36) and (43)

K_t = P_(t+h) / ( P_t · (1 + c_t)^h )

For an asset whose whole benefit is its later price, the later price discounted at c is the fair value in hindsight, and the price on the date is the valuation. The article's illustration reports K − 1 as the "raw gain".

What the numbers are not

They describe the inputs. K says how far two views are apart at a given c; it does not say which view is right. The price-history reading needs the later price, so it cannot be computed for today. Nothing here is a forecast or a recommendation.
